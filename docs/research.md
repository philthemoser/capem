# Research: the evidence CAPEM rests on

CAPEM (Centros de Apoyo en Emergencias / Centros de Apoio em Emergências / Emergency Support Centres) is an open concept prototype for coordinating improvised disaster support centres: the churches, schools and community halls that become shelters and collection points in the first days of a disaster.

This document sets out the evidence the design rests on. Three rules govern it:

1. Every figure carries its source, its publisher and its date.
2. Where a source hedged, we keep the hedge. A "rough estimate" is reported as a rough estimate.
3. Where the evidence is weak, we say so rather than dressing it up. Each section carries a confidence rating and a one-line reason.

If you have run a relief centre and something here does not match what you saw, we would rather be corrected than believed. See "Where this evidence is thin" at the end.

---

## 1. Material convergence and the "second disaster"

### The phenomenon has a name and a literature

"Material convergence" is the established term for the uncoordinated flow of goods towards a disaster site. Its consequence, when the flow exceeds what can be sorted and distributed, is widely called the "second disaster" or "the disaster within the disaster".

The clearest research statement is from the Natural Hazards Center at the University of Colorado Boulder, which describes it as "the disaster that follows the disaster", covering "the challenges of sorting and distributing donations as well as disposing of inappropriate donations". Their account is based on interviews after Hurricane Sandy (2012) and the 2013 Oklahoma tornadoes; one volunteer described the intake as "a yard sale dump".[^1]

The same framing appears in US state emergency-management doctrine. Washington State's *Volunteer and Donations Management Toolkit* warns that "if not properly managed, donations can become the disaster within the disaster".[^2] Holguín-Veras and colleagues use the cognate term "second-tier disaster".[^3] NPR popularised the phrase for a general audience in 2013.[^4]

**One correction worth making, because practitioners will check it.** FEMA's own doctrine does not use the phrase. The *Volunteer and Donations Management Support Annex* to the National Response Framework treats unaffiliated volunteers and unsolicited goods as a coordination problem, not a disaster: "Although unaffiliated volunteers can be significant resources, because they do not have preestablished relationships with emergency response organizations, verifying their training or credentials and matching them with the appropriate service areas can be difficult."[^5] Do not attribute "second disaster" to FEMA.

A second citation risk: USAID CIDI did much to popularise this framing, including its Greatest Good Donation Calculator.[^6] USAID was formally closed on 1 July 2025,[^7] and cidi.org now reads as a stale legacy site. Cite CIDI material with an access date and an archival note, or use the Natural Hazards Center instead.

### The 50 to 70 per cent figure, and what it actually says

The widely repeated claim that most donated goods in a disaster are unwanted traces to a single passage:

> "Recent rough estimates indicate that about 5 to 15 percent of the cargo arriving at the site consists of high-priority supplies, about 25 to 35 percent are low-priority supplies, and nonpriority supplies make up a staggering 50 to 70 percent."[^3]

Three things must travel with that number.

**It is an estimate, not a measurement.** The authors write "recent rough estimates indicate". No dataset, sample or set of events is given for the split. The peer-reviewed work by the same team does not carry the percentages: neither the 2012 *Journal of Operations Management* paper[^8] nor the 2014 *Natural Hazards Review* paper[^9] quantifies it, and nor does Paul's 2019 review chapter.[^10] The figure lives only in the practitioner-facing *TR News* article.

**"Non-priority" is broader than "unwanted".** The category comes from PAHO's classification, reproduced in the 2012 paper. Non-priority goods are those that "(1) are inappropriate for the event, time, context, population; (2) arrive unsorted or in a condition impossible to efficiently inventory/identify; (3) have surpassed expiry dates, are perishable, or are in poor condition; (4) arrive without a known or appropriate site for efficient distribution; and (5) are useless or of doubtful value".[^8] Items 2 and 4 are logistics failures, not donor failures. A useful blanket that arrives unlabelled in an unsorted sack, at a centre that cannot identify it, counts as non-priority.

That distinction matters to CAPEM directly. A meaningful share of the problem is not that people send the wrong things. It is that the right things arrive in a form nobody can process at speed.

**It applies to catastrophic events, not "a typical disaster".** The context in the source is major catastrophes such as Haiti 2010 and Tōhoku 2011.

**Do not cite the downstream versions.** Good360's blog headlines "up to 60% of goods donated during times of disaster go to waste" with no citation of any kind.[^11] Popular restatements also drop the hedge. These are the unsourced repetitions that make the underlying research look weaker than it is.

**How we phrase it in CAPEM materials:**

> In catastrophic events, researchers estimate that only 5 to 15 per cent of arriving relief cargo is high-priority, while 50 to 70 per cent is "non-priority": inappropriate, unsortable, expired, or with no identified destination. This is a published expert estimate rather than a measured statistic.

> **Confidence: MODERATE.** The phenomenon is strongly evidenced across independent research; the specific percentages rest on one hedged estimate that the peer-reviewed literature has never reproduced.

## 2. Coordination in practice

### Mexico City, 19 September 2017

The volunteer response to the 19S earthquake was coordinated substantially through WhatsApp groups and citizen-built platforms. The best documented of these is **Verificado19S**.

What it was, confirmed across four independent sources:

- **Origin and speed.** It grew out of a WhatsApp group of activists, journalists and programmers, and was operating within hours. Volunteers convened at Centro Cultural Horizontal in Colonia Roma from Wednesday 20 September, the day after the quake. *El Financiero* reports mobilisation "en cuestión de horas" and more than 100 volunteers: developers, designers, economists, mathematicians, anthropologists and psychologists.[^12] IJNet credits journalist Sandra Barrón with starting the fact-checking effort.[^13] Named organisers include Gabriela Márquez, Miguel Escalante and Gisela Pérez.
- **Technology.** It ran on Google Crisis Map, not Ushahidi. Google supplied engineers on site and remotely, along with data architecture and cloud capacity. Inputs came from web forms, Twitter, Waze, Codeando México and Mexico City's C5 emergency centre. Field volunteers reported through WhatsApp groups.[^12][^14]
- **Method.** Nothing was published unless verified by telephone with at least two independent on-site sources.[^14]
- **What it mapped.** Collapsed buildings, shelters, collection centres, and crucially the *specific unmet needs* at each. Later it added #V19sBuscandonos for missing persons.[^13]
- **Scale.** Around 40 volunteers staffed the central verification hub at any time, out of more than 100 in total; roughly 36,000 Twitter followers; an update cycle of about 15 minutes.[^12][^14][^15]
- **Partners.** Article 19, Horizontal, CartoCrítica, Cultura Colectiva, Data Cívica, SocialTIC, Oxfam, Vice, Google and McKinsey, among others.

Peer-reviewed corroboration exists. Mora, Saffon and Gómez document a Verificado19S WhatsApp channel with "más de ochenta miembros" enabling "el envío de mensajes en tiempo real sobre necesidades urgentes", and brigadistas routing needs "a un formulario en línea diseñado por Verificado19S, que a su vez lo enviaba a sus chats de acopio".[^16]

**Two corrections.** Verificado19S was a citizen-led verification and mapping platform, not a donation-matching system and not a government system. Formal search and rescue was run by the Navy, the army and Protección Civil. It is accurate to say the *volunteer and civil-society* response ran on WhatsApp and crowdsourced platforms; it is not accurate to say the response as a whole did.

### Volunteer convergence in Mexico City: what we can and cannot claim

We had intended to claim that far more spontaneous volunteers arrived than could be used. The evidence does not support that in the strong form.

What exists:

- Northeastern University's Global Resilience Institute notes that "some organizations have even turned away volunteers". That is a single clause with no elaboration.[^17]
- *The New Humanitarian*, 21 September 2017, documents localised saturation rather than global surplus. Coordinator Jorge Suarez: "We just found out about two [more] buildings that crumbled. So we are trying to tell volunteers to go there. We have plenty of people here already." A rescue official at another site: "At this site, in Colonia Roma, we already have enough food, for example. Now we need to get more ladders for our rescue teams."[^18]

What cuts the other way:

- Contemporaneous reporting emphasises shortages. Volunteers "begged for headlamps" as night fell.[^19]
- The main sociological study of the response describes *underserved* zones, quoting a participant on "el único lugar que conocíamos en la ciudad adonde no había llegado acopio", and frames the citizen networks as coordinating successfully rather than being swamped.[^16]
- The medical after-action study of the response does not discuss volunteer excess at all.[^20]

**How we phrase it:**

> Volunteer convergence is a well-established disaster phenomenon. In Mexico City in 2017 it produced acute *distributional* imbalance: some sites had more volunteers and supplies than they could use while others went unserved, and some organisations turned volunteers away. Coordinators spent effort redirecting people between sites.

For the phenomenon in general, cite the volunteer-convergence literature rather than a Mexico-specific quantification we cannot source.[^21]

> **Confidence: STRONG** on Verificado19S (four independent sources including peer-reviewed work). **WEAK** on volunteer over-convergence in this event: the direct evidence is two passing remarks, and the main academic study points the other way.

### Rio Grande do Sul, April to May 2024

Two distinct official platforms operated, and they are often confused:

1. **SOS Rio Grande do Sul**, the state emergency portal at `sosenchentes.rs.gov.br`, built by PROCERGS, the state ICT company. Its published sections confirm the functions: shelter monitoring (`/abrigos`), how to help a shelter (`/como-ajudar-um-abrigo`), the official Pix campaign (`/sobre-o-pix`) and animal assistance.
2. **Solidariedade RS**, a later platform under the Plano Rio Grande recovery programme and the state innovation secretariat (SICT), specifically for connecting shelters and institutions with donations. Confirmed by official page titles on two state domains: "Abrigos e instituições já estão recebendo doações pela plataforma Solidariedade RS".

A third, citizen-built platform is worth noting as a close analogue to Verificado19S: **Salva RS**, created by volunteers, converted emergency requests into geolocated points with mapped routes and had assisted in more than 12,000 rescues by 9 May 2024.[^22]

**The clothing oversupply.** By mid-May 2024, clothing accounted for 70 per cent of all donations collected nationally for Rio Grande do Sul. Correios, the federal postal service, suspended accepting clothing on 15 May 2024 on that basis, stating "As peças de vestuário já correspondem a 70% dos donativos arrecadados", and asked the public to "dê prioridade a itens como água potável, alimentos não perecíveis, ração para animais, material de limpeza e de higiene pessoal". By that date around 11,000 tonnes of donations had been collected at post offices nationwide, of which roughly 3,000 tonnes had been delivered to the RS Civil Defense for distribution to 839 registered shelters.[^23]

Relief coordinators had made the same appeal a week earlier. Patrícia Seixas of Liga do Bem: "Fica o pedido para as pessoas diminuírem a quantidade de roupa doada, é o que toma mais tempo." Rodrigo Afonso of Ação da Cidadania: "O que sempre sobra são as roupas... Não precisa mais de roupa."[^24]

**Attribution correction.** The public request to stop sending clothing came from Correios and from NGO relief coordinators, not from a Civil Defense statement. We have not located a Civil Defense statement in those words. Attribute it correctly.

**On the claim that donated clothing became a textile-waste problem: we have dropped it.** We found no authoritative source documenting donated clothing entering a waste stream in Rio Grande do Sul in 2024. The most widely circulated apparent evidence, a video of a backhoe moving donated clothing, was fact-checked as false: it was footage from Encantado in September 2023 recirculated in 2024, and the municipality stated "nenhuma roupa foi jogada fora", explaining the machinery was being used to create storage space at a sorting centre.[^25] The 80 per cent textile-discard figure circulating in Brazilian media is a national fast-fashion statistic unrelated to the floods.[^26] Do not import it.

What is well evidenced is oversupply and sorting burden, not disposal. That is what we claim.

> **Confidence: STRONG** on the clothing oversupply figures and the Correios suspension (federal news agency, precise figures and dates). **MODERATE** on the platform landscape: names and owners are confirmed, but see "What we could not verify".

## 3. Cash and payment rails

Brazil's instant payment system, Pix, was the state's promoted cash donation channel in 2024, and it moved money at a speed that goods logistics cannot match.

- The state government created an official Pix key on Friday 3 May 2024, on a Banrisul account, explicitly to direct donations to an official channel and reduce fraud.
- R$38.2 million had been received by 5 May, two days later.[^27]
- More than R$101 million by 15 May.[^22]
- A Comitê Gestor was constituted to set distribution criteria.
- Private partners amplified it heavily. Uber placed the official SOS RS Pix key (CNPJ 92.958.800/0001-38) on its app homepage and funded free rides to collection points in four cities.[^28]

Two precisions matter. Pix was promoted as the official *cash* channel, created chiefly as an anti-fraud measure, and the state did not position it as a replacement for goods drives, which ran in parallel throughout. And R$101 million in twelve days is a fact about payment rails, not about whether cash was the right modality; that is a separate argument with its own literature.

For CAPEM the relevant lesson is narrower and more useful: where an instant, low-friction, nationally trusted rail exists, cash reaches the response fast and at scale, while goods remain slow, heavy, and dependent on the sorting capacity of whoever receives them. A design that handles goods should assume cash is already handled better elsewhere and not compete with it.

> **Confidence: STRONG.** Figures come from state government releases and mainstream Brazilian press, with dates and named accounts.

## 4. Shelter infrastructure and who actually runs it

In the 2024 Rio Grande do Sul floods, 803 shelters operated at peak, including 79 state schools. Around 18,000 people were in shelters at any given time, with a census counting 75,000 shelter residents in total. Of the 803 shelters, 543 had running water and 516 had kitchen facilities.[^22] That last pair of numbers is the important one: a large share of the shelter estate was buildings never designed to shelter anyone.

Post-event research supports the pattern. Figueira, Lyszkowski and Branco studied 28 shelters in Porto Alegre housing 3,199 people and conclude that "centros comunitários e igrejas são as melhores edificações pré-existentes para acolhimento nessas condições, pela flexibilidade dos usos dos espaços", while noting real limitations: "ventilação inadequada, escassez de banheiros".[^29]

Individual parishes carried substantial loads. Nossa Senhora de Fátima in Guaíba sheltered 220 people plus 80 more at an associated community; Nossa Senhora da Glória in Cascata, Porto Alegre, 311 across five sites; Santa Luzia in Canoas around 300, later 130; São Jorge in Partenon took 60 oncology patients.[^30]

**Correction, and it matters for how CAPEM positions itself.** We had intended to claim these became government-recognised shelter infrastructure with civil defense routing displaced people to them. The only source that speaks directly to the question contradicts the second half. A parish priest in the same report states plainly: "We did not receive any help from public authorities." Those shelters ran on parishioner donations and volunteers.[^30] The academic study does not distinguish official from spontaneous shelters. We found no source showing civil defense systematically routing people to church buildings.

**How we phrase it:**

> In the 2024 Rio Grande do Sul floods, 803 shelters operated at peak, the great majority in buildings never designed for the purpose. Churches and community centres carried a substantial share, and post-event research identifies them as the most suitable pre-existing building types given their spatial flexibility, with individual parishes sheltering hundreds of people each. Many operated largely on their own resources; some parish shelters reported receiving no assistance from public authorities.

That is a weaker claim than "recognised infrastructure", and a better argument for CAPEM. The gap is not that these centres were absorbed into the official system. It is that they were doing the work outside it, without tooling.

> **Confidence: MODERATE.** Shelter counts and the building-type finding are well sourced. Coordination between improvised shelters and civil defense is documented in one direction only, from a single report, and needs practitioner input.

## 5. Data protection

The full position is in [`docs/data-protection.md`](./data-protection.md). In summary:

The ICRC's *Handbook on Data Protection in Humanitarian Action*, third edition, November 2024, published with the Brussels Privacy Hub by Cambridge University Press, sets out in Chapter 3 why consent is frequently not an appropriate legal basis for processing the personal data of people receiving humanitarian assistance. Consent is not valid where "the Data Subject has no genuine and free choice, or is unable to refuse or withdraw Consent without detriment", a problem sharpened when "consenting to the Processing of Personal Data is a precondition to receive assistance".[^31]

The Handbook offers three alternative bases: vital interest, important grounds of public interest, and legitimate interest.

The Handbook does **not** say consent is never valid. Write "often not an appropriate legal basis", not "invalid". Practitioners will check.

CALP Network material supports the ethical framing, notably Amos Doornbos: "It's hard to call it consent when it's a requirement for participation, when there are not meaningful alternatives to digital registration available."[^32] Cite CALP for that framing only; the blog does not discuss legal bases.

> **Confidence: STRONG.** Primary source, current edition, quoted directly with section references.

## 6. The existing landscape

CAPEM's signature proposed mechanism is that donors pre-register individual bags or boxes and present a machine-readable tag at drop-off, so intake becomes a scan and centres can steer donations before they arrive.

We searched for prior implementations in English, Spanish, Portuguese and German. **We found no published disaster-relief platform that does this.** But that claim needs narrowing, because there are two things close enough that any practitioner will raise them, and we would rather raise them first.

### The real precedent: Aidmatrix and the National Donations Management Network

The NDMN, powered by Aidmatrix, was a virtual portal where donors could post offers of goods, financial contributions or volunteer services online, while receiving organisations posted specific needs and could accept donor offers, with delivery coordinated afterwards. State emergency-management doctrine is explicit that this meant goods were matched to stated needs *before* being shipped, rather than arriving unsolicited.[^2]

That is genuine donor-side pre-registration of goods matched against needs, and it predates CAPEM by well over a decade.

What it was not: registration at the level of the individual physical container, with a machine-readable tag on that container, scanned at a drop-off point. It operated at the level of the offer, organisation to organisation, oriented towards freight. It was closer to a structured want-ads board than to airline check-in.

It also appears to be defunct. The aidmatrixnetwork.org domain still carries NDMN marketing copy but has been repurposed as an affiliate product-review site, last updated October 2022. Do not describe NDMN in the present tense, and be ready to explain why it lapsed.

### The commercial near-miss: ReSupply

ReSupply is a US furniture and home-goods donation marketplace where an individual donor books a collection online and itemises what they are giving.[^33] That is donor-side item registration, live today. It is a pickup model rather than a drop-off model, has no tag on the container, and is not a disaster tool. But it establishes that donors will itemise goods in advance when the interface is easy.

### The inverted case: Spenden-Verteilzentrum Ahrtal

After the 2021 Ahr valley floods in Germany, the distribution centre ran an online portal. It is worth studying, and it is the mirror image of CAPEM's proposal: the portal serves *recipients*. Flood-affected people register with proof of being affected, browse a catalogue, reserve items and collect them. "Die Bedarfsermittlung wurde digitalisiert." Donors use separate channels.[^34]

### How we state the differentiation

> We found no published disaster-relief platform that has donors register individual bags or boxes in advance and present a machine-readable tag on that container at a drop-off point. The nearest precedent is the Aidmatrix-powered National Donations Management Network, which from the late 2000s let donors post goods offers online for agencies to accept before shipment. That was pre-registration at the level of the offer rather than the container, with no tagging and no scan-in, and it now appears defunct. In the commercial sector, ReSupply lets individual donors itemise goods online, but for scheduled collections rather than disaster drop-offs.

What is new in CAPEM is therefore not the idea of registering donations before they move. It is the unit of registration (the individual container), the artefact (a scannable tag on that container), and the moment (scan-in at the receiving centre).

> **Confidence: MODERATE.** The search was systematic and multilingual, but see the absence-of-evidence limit below.

## What we could not verify

Two gaps that a reader should know about.

**The Solidariedade RS workflow.** At the time of this research, every `rs.gov.br` page we attempted returned either a 404 or a 302 redirect to `estado.rs.gov.br/periodo-eleitoral`, a Brazilian electoral-period content blackout. Both the Internet Archive and archive.today were unreachable from our research environment. The platform's *name, owning agencies and stated purpose* are confirmed from indexed official page titles on two state domains and from third-party coverage. Its actual matching mechanics are not. We have not described them, and the table entry above is marked accordingly. Anyone with a snapshot, a screenshot, or first-hand experience of using it should tell us; it may well be closer to CAPEM's concept than we can currently see.

**The absence-of-evidence limit on the landscape claim.** Much disaster-response tooling is unpublished, built by local government, never written up in English, or lives inside an NGO and never reaches the web. A claim that "no platform does X" is stronger than public sources can support. We therefore write "we found no published implementation of", and we treat every practitioner who says "we built something like that in 2019" as a correction rather than an inconvenience. This is the single claim where being wrong would matter most to the project, and where a false negative is worse for us than an awkward finding.

## Where this evidence is thin: help wanted

CAPEM is a concept prototype. The evidence above is assembled from the outside, from published sources, by people reading about relief centres rather than running them. That is the weakest part of the whole project, and it is the repo's actual ask.

We would particularly like to be corrected on the following.

**Whether the intake bottleneck is really where we think it is.** Our design assumes that sorting and identification at the receiving centre is the chokepoint, on the strength of the PAHO non-priority definition and the Correios clothing figures. If the real bottleneck at your centre was transport, storage, staffing at the door, or getting goods back out again, the design is aimed at the wrong problem.

**Volunteer convergence.** Our evidence for over-convergence in Mexico City 2017 is two passing remarks in press coverage, and the main academic study of that response describes underserved areas instead. If you have run intake at a site where more volunteers arrived than you could use, or where the opposite was true, that is worth more than what we have.

**Whether improvised centres want to be coordinated at all.** The clearest data point we have on church-run shelters in Rio Grande do Sul is a priest saying they received no help from public authorities. That could mean the coordination gap is a problem to solve. It could equally mean these centres function precisely because they sit outside official systems, and that a tool which makes them legible to the state is a liability rather than a service. We do not know which. This is a question about power, not software, and we are not in a position to answer it.

**Whether donors would actually pre-register anything.** ReSupply suggests people will itemise goods for a booked collection. Nobody has shown they will do it for a drop-off during a disaster, at 6am, on a phone, in a queue.

**Anything resembling the tag-at-drop-off mechanism that we missed.** See the absence-of-evidence note above. If it exists and failed, why it failed is the most valuable thing anyone could contribute to this repo.

Open an issue, or send a correction with a source. Sources beat recollections, but recollections from people who were there beat our reading, and we will record them as such.
